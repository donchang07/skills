# Academic Literature on Organizational AI Maturity / Readiness / Capability Measurement (for AX multi-respondent assessment design)

> Method note: Research was done on 2026-10-01 with web search only. Most publisher and repository pages (peerj.com, aisel.aisnet.org, sciencedirect, semanticscholar, eprints.qut, bcg.com) were blocked by the egress proxy, and the Firecrawl scraper was rate-limited. So the bibliographic facts below come from search-result abstracts and snippets, not full texts. **No item wording from any scale has been verified.** Treat any statistic marked "(snippet)" as needing a check against the original before it goes into the app or a client deliverable.

## 1. Systematic reviews and scholarly AI maturity / readiness models

### Takeaway
The scholarly AI maturity and readiness literature is mostly qualitative and design-science work: SLRs, expert interviews and DSR artifacts. These sources give dimensions, factors and indicators. They do not give psychometrically validated survey items. Across sources the dimensions converge on: strategy/alignment, data, technology/infrastructure, people/skills/knowledge, culture/organization/change, governance/ethics, and (for maturity) processes/use cases.

### Cited Findings
- **Sadiq, Safie, Abd Rahman & Goudarzi (2021)**, "Artificial intelligence maturity model: a systematic literature review," *PeerJ Computer Science* 7:e661, DOI 10.7717/peerj-cs.661. It reviews AI maturity model studies. From 13 studies it identifies these critical success factors / most critical dimensions: **Data, Analytics, Technology & Tools, Intelligent Automation, Governance, People, Organization** — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-661/); [PMC mirror](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8409328/)
- **Jöhnk, Weißert & Wyrtki (2021)**, "Ready or Not, AI Comes — An Interview Study of Organizational AI Readiness Factors," *Business & Information Systems Engineering* 63(1): 5–20. The study is qualitative, with **25 interviews with AI experts**. It found **18 AI readiness factors with 58 indicators** in **5 categories: strategic alignment, resources, knowledge, culture, data**. The stated aim is to help organizations determine their AI readiness level, identify gaps and make informed adoption decisions — [AISeL](https://aisel.aisnet.org/bise/vol63/iss1/2/); [Fraunhofer Publica](https://publica.fraunhofer.de/entities/publication/614e1c81-77ad-4d9f-88f3-c5f2bdb58860)
- **Alsheibani, Cheung & Messom (2018)**, "Artificial Intelligence Adoption: AI-readiness at Firm-Level," *PACIS 2018* (Yokohama). It frames firm-level AI readiness using TOE and Diffusion of Innovation — [AISeL](https://aisel.aisnet.org/pacis2018/37/); [Monash](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-adoption-ai-readiness-at-firm-level/)
- **Pumplun, Tauchert & Heidt (2019)**, "A New Organizational Chassis for Artificial Intelligence — Exploring Organizational Readiness Factors," *ECIS 2019* (Stockholm & Uppsala). It extends the TOE framework for AI and gives **12 propositions**. It highlights data availability, quality and protection, and regulation (GDPR) — [AISeL](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2019_rp/106/)
- **Holmström (2022)**, "From AI to digital transformation: The AI readiness framework," *Business Horizons* 65(3): 329–339. This is a conceptual AI readiness framework that links AI to digital transformation (dimensions not verified; abstract not accessible) — [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/4roGr5el/). A later *Business Horizons* paper, "Navigating the organizational AI journey: The AI transformation framework" (2025), also appeared — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0007681325000023) (authors and content not verified)
- **Fukas, Rebstadt, Remark & Thomas (2021)**, "Developing an Artificial Intelligence Maturity Model for Auditing," *ECIS 2021*. Built with DSR: SLR, a qualitative survey of audit experts and iterative development. It has **8 dimensions × 5 maturity levels** — [AISeL](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2021_rp/133/)
- **Banh et al. (2025)**, "Developing a Generative AI Maturity Model for Supporting the Organizational Adoption Journey," WIP paper at *Wirtschaftsinformatik 2025* (Münster). Built with DSR from literature and expert interviews. The preliminary model has dimensions **people, process, technology, data, organization** across **5 levels**. It argues that general AI maturity models miss GenAI-specific characteristics — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393412405_Developing_a_Generative_AI_Maturity_Model_for_Supporting_the_Organizational_Adoption_Journey); [Uni Duisburg-Essen](https://www.ris.uni-due.de/en/research/publications/developing-a-generative-ai-maturity-model-for-supporting-the-organizational-adoption-journey-17452/)
- Also surfaced (title only, content not verified): "Organizational Readiness for Generative AI Adoption," *ECIS 2025* — [AISeL](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2025/ai_org/ai_org/12/); "Understanding Artificial Intelligence Diffusion through an AI Capability Maturity Model," *Information Systems Frontiers* (2024), DOI 10.1007/s10796-024-10528-4 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10796-024-10528-4); SLRs on Responsible-AI maturity models — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S266709682300040X)

### Inferences
- Jöhnk et al.'s 5 categories, 18 factors and 58 indicators are the most granular scholarly scaffold for an organizational *readiness* question bank. The indicators could be turned into Likert items, but they were not designed or validated as survey items.
- A GenAI-era AX survey should add GenAI-specific content (use of LLM tools, prompt/agent workflows, GenAI governance). Banh et al. explicitly argue that classic AI maturity models under-cover this.
- The overlap between Sadiq's 7 dimensions, Jöhnk's 5 categories and Banh's 5 dimensions supports a core set of roughly 6–8 AX dimensions.

### Gaps
- **Ellefsen et al. (2019)** (AI maturity in Nordic/logistics context) was not searched or verified.
- Full dimension lists for Sadiq (frequencies, level counts) and Holmström (framework dimensions and levels) could not be read.
- None of these models reports psychometric validation such as CFA or reliability of a survey instrument, as far as abstracts show.

## 2. Validated measurement scales (organizational AI capability, adoption, employee AI literacy, GenAI)

### Takeaway
The most-cited *validated* organizational instrument is Mikalef & Gupta's (2021) AI capability scale: 3 second-order formative dimensions over 8 first-order constructs. For employees, the validated AI literacy scales are Wang et al. (2023) and Pinski & Benlian (2023). Their exact items need to be obtained from the full papers. Do not paraphrase them from memory.

### Cited Findings
- **Mikalef & Gupta (2021)**, "Artificial intelligence capability: Conceptualization, measurement calibration, and empirical study on its impact on organizational creativity and firm performance," *Information & Management* 58(3): 103434, DOI 10.1016/j.im.2021.103434 — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378720621000082); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348738492)
  - AI capability is defined as "a firm's ability to structure, bundle, and leverage its AI-based resources." It has **three second-order formative dimensions over eight first-order constructs**:
    - **Tangible**: data, technology, basic resources
    - **Human**: technical skills, business skills
    - **Intangible**: inter-departmental coordination, organizational change capacity, risk proclivity

    Source: [search synthesis of ScienceDirect, Birmingham, Umeå sources](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/files/219095410/1467-8551.12797.pdf)
  - Sample size, item wording and fit statistics were **not verified** (no full-text access).
  - A widely reused follow-on applies the scale in public organizations — [Tampere repository](https://researchportal.tuni.fi/files/84905564/Examining_how_AI_capabilities.pdf)
- **Wang, Rau & Yuan (2023)**, "Measuring user competence in using artificial intelligence: validity and reliability of artificial intelligence literacy scale," *Behaviour & Information Technology* 42(9): 1324–1337. Its dimensions are **Awareness, Usage, Evaluation, Ethics** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360519116). Note: one search snippet gave a different 3-factor, 16-item structure for "the AI literacy scale." That snippet appears to conflate a different scale, so the item count and statistics are **not verified**.
- **Pinski & Benlian (2023)**, "AI Literacy — Towards Measuring Human Competency in Artificial Intelligence," *HICSS-56*. It measures AI literacy of non-experts in a work context and distinguishes explicit knowledge from tacit, experience-based knowledge. Snippet-level claims: development used **two card-sorting rounds (6 and 5 judges)** and a **pre-test with 50 participants** (snippet, not verified) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370210898)
- Other AI literacy instruments that surfaced (not reviewed in depth):
  - MAILS (Meta AI Literacy Scale) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949882123000142)
  - SNAIL (non-experts' AI literacy) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958823000714)
  - AICOS (objective AI competency) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.12921)
  - GLAT (GenAI literacy test) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00283)
  - A systematic review of AI literacy scales in *npj Science of Learning* (2024) — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-024-00264-4)
  - A paper on misalignment between self-reported and objective AI literacy measures — [arXiv 2601.06101](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.06101)
- **TOE-based adoption factors**: Pumplun et al. (2019) and Alsheibani et al. (2018) above are conceptual and qualitative, not validated scales. One empirical TOE-style study surfaced: "Technology readiness and the organizational journey towards AI adoption," *International Journal of Information Management* 68 (2023), DOI 10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2022.102588 — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2022.102588) (authors and items not verified)

### Inferences
- A practical AX design would use Mikalef & Gupta's 8 constructs as the organization-level spine. These items are typically answered by managers or key informants.
- Employee-level modules could be adapted from Wang et al. (AILS) or Pinski & Benlian. If these are adapted, revalidate with pilot data, alpha and CFA. Self-report AI literacy may diverge from objective competence (arXiv 2601.06101), so consider adding a few knowledge or scenario items.
- Mikalef & Gupta's second-order constructs are *formative*. Formative indicators are not interchangeable, and Cronbach's alpha is not the appropriate check for them. This matters for how the app computes dimension scores.

### Gaps
- Baier et al. (AI adoption/readiness measure) was not found in this pass.
- A dedicated, peer-reviewed, validated *organizational GenAI adoption scale* was not identified. ECIS 2025 "Organizational Readiness for Generative AI Adoption" is a candidate but was not read.
- Exact items, loadings, AVE and CR for all scales were not verified.

## 3. Maturity model development methodology

### Takeaway
There are three canonical methods. De Bruin et al. (2005) give 6 phases: scope, design, populate, test, deploy, maintain. Becker, Knackstedt & Pöppelbuß (2009) give an 8-step DSR procedure model. Pöppelbuß & Röglinger (2011) give design principles grouped by purpose of use: descriptive, prescriptive, comparative. A recurring critique, e.g., Röglinger, Pöppelbuß & Becker (2012), is weak methodological transparency and weak empirical validation.

### Cited Findings
- **de Bruin, Freeze, Kulkarni & Rosemann (2005)**, "Understanding the Main Phases of Developing a Maturity Assessment Model," *ACIS 2005* (Sydney). It generalizes development phases across domains and is illustrated with BPM and Knowledge Management models. It was the first accumulated effort to generalize maturity model development phases — [AISeL](https://aisel.aisnet.org/acis2005/109/); [QUT ePrints](https://eprints.qut.edu.au/25152/). The commonly cited six phases (Scope → Design → Populate → Test → Deploy → Maintain) come from background knowledge; the full text was not read here.
- **Becker, Knackstedt & Pöppelbuß (2009)**, "Developing Maturity Models for IT Management — A Procedure Model and its Application," *BISE* 1(3): 213–222, DOI 10.1007/s12599-009-0044-5. It is a generic procedure model with **eight stages** aligned with Hevner et al.'s (2004) DSR guidelines — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12599-009-0044-5); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/binfse/v1y2009i3p213-222.html)
- **Pöppelbuß & Röglinger (2011)**, "What makes a useful maturity model? A framework of general design principles for maturity models and its demonstration in business process management," *ECIS 2011*. Its design principles are grouped by typical purposes of use and demonstrated on BPM maturity models. It is widely used as a checklist for methodological soundness — [AISeL](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2011/28/); [PDF](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1027&context=ecis2011)
- **Röglinger, Pöppelbuß & Becker (2012)**, "Maturity models in business process management," *Business Process Management Journal* 18(2): 328–346. It systematically reviews BPM maturity models against design principles. Later literature notes recurring weaknesses in methodological transparency, applicability and standardization — [T&F SLR on BPMM limitations](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10580530.2024.2332210)

### Inferences
- For AX, a recommended pipeline is:
  1. Scope the model with de Bruin.
  2. Document the development steps with Becker et al.'s 8 steps.
  3. Self-audit against Pöppelbuß & Röglinger's principles. For an assessment app, the descriptive principles are minimum; prescriptive ones (improvement roadmaps per level) add value.
- **Staged vs. continuous representation** (CMMI): a staged model assigns one overall maturity level. A continuous model assigns a capability level per dimension/process area. A multi-dimension survey app maps naturally to the continuous approach (per-dimension profile), with an optional derived overall stage.

### Gaps
- The CMMI staged/continuous description is from background knowledge. No CMMI Institute or SEI source was retrieved, so cite the CMMI for Development v1.3 technical report (SEI, 2010) after verifying.
- The exact names of Becker et al.'s 8 steps and Pöppelbuß & Röglinger's individual principles were not retrieved.

## 4. Survey methodology for multi-respondent organizational assessment

### Takeaway
Multi-informant designs mitigate key-informant and common-method bias. Aggregating individual responses to the unit level requires agreement and reliability evidence (rwg(j), ICC(1), ICC(2)), interpreted on graded rather than dichotomous cutoffs. Practitioner data (BCG) show a large leader-vs-frontline gap in GenAI use, so role-segmented reporting is essential.

### Cited Findings
- **Kumar, Stern & Anderson (1993)**, "Conducting Interorganizational Research Using Key Informants," *Academy of Management Journal* 36(6): 1633–1651. It notes the field still relies on single informants despite advocacy for multiple informants, and addresses informant selection and obtaining perceptual agreement among multiple informants — [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/256824); [SMU](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/5176/)
- **Wagner, Rau & Lindemann (2010)**, "Multiple Informant Methodology: A Critical Review and Recommendations," *Sociological Methods & Research* — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0049124110366231) (content not read). Also "Using Multi-Informant Designs to Address Key Informant and Common Method Bias" (Springer chapter) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-8349-3722-3_4)
- **Enticott et al. (2004)**, "Multiple Voices of Modernization: Some Methodological Implications," *Public Administration* — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.0033-3298.2004.00417.x). This is a known study showing that responses about organizational practices differ systematically by organizational echelon; details not verified here.
- **Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee & Podsakoff (2003)**, "Common Method Biases in Behavioral Research: A Critical Review of the Literature and Recommended Remedies," *Journal of Applied Psychology* 88(5): 879–903. It reviews sources of method bias and evaluates procedural and statistical remedies — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279796507). See also the *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology* piece "Common Method Bias: It's Bad, It's Complex, It's Widespread, and It's Not Easy to Fix" — [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-110721-040030)
- **LeBreton & Senter (2008)**, "Answers to 20 Questions About Interrater Reliability and Interrater Agreement," *Organizational Research Methods* — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234021681); summary via [Frontiers overview](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00777/full) and search synthesis:
  - They argue the rwg ≥ .70 cutoff "artificially dichotomizes agreement." Graded bands: .00–.30 lack of agreement; .31–.50 weak; .51–.70 moderate; .71–.90 strong; .91–1.00 very strong.
  - ICC(1) should be interpreted as an effect size: .01 small, .10 medium, .25 large. ICC(2) around .70 is suggested.
- **Biemann, Cole & Voelpel (2012)**, "Within-group agreement: On the use (and misuse) of rWG and rWG(J) in leadership research and some best practice guidelines," *The Leadership Quarterly* — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229313007) (content not read). Also "Justifying Aggregation With Consensus-Based Constructs: A Review and Examination of Cutoff Values for Common Aggregation Indices" (Woehr et al., *Organizational Research Methods*, 2015) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276529435) (not read)
- **Perception and usage gap (practitioner evidence)**, from BCG "AI at Work 2025: Momentum Builds, but Gaps Remain" (June 2025), with **10,635 employees across 11 nations** (secondary reports):
  - More than three-quarters of leaders and managers use GenAI several times a week, while regular use among frontline employees **stalled at 51%** (a "silicon ceiling").
  - Managers' regular use rose to 78% (from 64% in 2023 and 46% in 2018).
  - Overall, 72% are regular users.

  Sources: [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/ai-at-work-momentum-builds-but-gaps-remain); [UNLEASH](https://www.unleash.ai/artificial-intelligence/analysis/bcgs-ai-at-work-2025-report-four-takeaways-for-hr-leaders); [AI Magazine](https://aimagazine.com/news/bcgs-findings-on-the-gen-ai-problem-explained). The bcg.com page itself could not be fetched; figures come from secondary coverage and search snippets.

### Inferences
- AX app design implications:
  - Collect role and level (exec/manager/frontline) and function for each respondent.
  - Compute unit scores only when the unit meets an agreement threshold. Use graded rwg(j) bands, and report ICC(1)/ICC(2) across units when there are enough units.
  - Show executive-vs-employee gaps as a first-class output, not as noise. The BCG gap and the Enticott-style echelon effects imply the disagreement itself is diagnostic.
- Procedural remedies for common method bias (Podsakoff) that fit an app:
  - Anonymity assurances
  - Separating predictor and outcome items, or splitting sources (e.g., execs rate strategy and employees rate usage)
  - Varied scale anchors
  - Randomized item order
- On minimum respondents per unit: ICC(2) depends on group size, so small units (e.g., fewer than 3–5 respondents) will yield unstable unit means. A minimum of about 3 is commonly used in practice, but **no authoritative source was retrieved here**.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source was retrieved for a specific minimum respondents-per-unit rule.
- No academic (vs. consultancy) study quantifying the executive-vs-employee perception gap specifically on AI readiness or maturity was found.

## 5. Reliability and validity steps (content validity, pilot, alpha, CFA)

### Takeaway
The standard sequence is:
1. Item generation from the literature (e.g., Jöhnk indicators, Mikalef constructs)
2. Expert content validation (I-CVI ≥ .78 with 6+ experts; S-CVI/Ave ≥ .90), optionally preceded by card sorting (as in Pinski & Benlian)
3. Cognitive and pilot testing
4. EFA and reliability (alpha/omega for reflective scales)
5. CFA on a separate sample
6. Aggregation checks for unit-level scores

### Cited Findings
- **Lynn (1986)** and **Polit & Beck (2006)**, "The Content Validity Index: Are you sure you know what's being reported? Critique and recommendations," *Research in Nursing & Health* 29: 489–497 — [PDF](https://faculty.ksu.edu.sa/sites/default/files/the_content_validity_index_are_you_sure_1.pdf):
  - With six or more judges, I-CVI should be no lower than .78. Excellent content validity means S-CVI/Ave ≥ .90.
  - Lynn advised a minimum of 3 experts and said more than 10 was probably unnecessary.

  Search synthesis of the above; see also [metricgate calculator doc](https://metricgate.com/docs/content-validity-index-cvi/)
- **Polit, Beck & Owen (2007)**, "Is the CVI an acceptable indicator of content validity? Appraisal and recommendations" (a kappa-adjusted CVI for chance agreement) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6185294) (not read in full)
- Pinski & Benlian (2023) used card sorting with expert judges and a pre-test of 50 before validation (snippet) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370210898)
- Wang et al. (2023) AILS reports CFA-based validity and reliability (title states "validity and reliability"; statistics not verified) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360519116)

### Inferences
- For the AX app, build in an "expert review mode" to collect relevance ratings on a 4-point scale and auto-compute I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave.
- Pilot with roughly 30–50 respondents before full deployment.
- Store item-level responses so that alpha/omega, EFA/CFA and rwg/ICC can be computed later.
- Use alpha only for reflective constructs. Mikalef & Gupta's formative higher-order constructs need weights and collinearity (VIF) checks instead.

### Gaps
- Specific CFA fit thresholds (e.g., Hu & Bentler 1999 CFI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .06) and alpha conventions (≥ .70, Nunnally) are standard background knowledge but were not retrieved and cited in this pass.
- No AI maturity model was found that reports the full CVI → pilot → CFA chain. This appears to be a genuine gap in the literature, so it is a differentiation opportunity for a rigorously validated AX instrument.
